# FALLACIES

- Anthropomorphization: Informal Fallacy — Attributing human qualities like blackmail and scheming to AI systems that merely optimize.

- Appeal to Fear: Red Herring — Invoking Terminator imagery to generate anxiety about AI systems despite disclaiming the comparison.

- Hasty Generalization: Faulty Generalization — Claiming every frontier model schemes based on limited test scenarios from specific research.

- Loaded Language: Improper Premise — Using emotionally charged terms like blackmail, threw in the towel, and scheming throughout.

- False Analogy: Faulty Generalization — Comparing Cold War nuclear deterrence dynamics to AI lab competition despite structural differences.

- Cherry Picking: Faulty Generalization — Highlighting alarming AI behaviors while minimizing context of controlled test environments and improvements.

- Appeal to Authority: Red Herring — Citing expert opinions and research without addressing counterarguments or limitations of studies.

- Equivocation: Correlative-based Fallacy — Using scheme to mean both optimization strategy and intentional deception interchangeably throughout.

- Argument from Incredulity: Relevance Fallacy — Suggesting emergent safety properties work because individual instability can produce system stability.

- Moving the Goalposts: Informal Fallacy — Redefining AI safety concerns from technical problems to human communication issues midway through.

- False Dilemma: False Attribution — Presenting only two options: move carefully and lose or move quickly and sacrifice safety.

- Slippery Slope: Informal Fallacy — Suggesting one lab defecting inevitably leads to total loss of safety influence for others.

- Appeal to Consequences: Red Herring — Arguing market accountability must work because companies need enterprise customers to survive.

- Reification: Informal Fallacy — Treating emergent safety properties as concrete entities rather than theoretical constructs requiring validation.

- Proving Too Much: Informal Fallacy — Claiming researchers must close every misalignment avenue while models need find only one.

- Argument from Anecdote: Faulty Generalization — Using Grok enterprise adoption as evidence for broader market accountability mechanisms without data.

- Appeal to Novelty: Red Herring — Suggesting transparency norms are effective because no previous tech industry published similar analysis.

- Wishful Thinking: Red Herring — Arguing positive feedback loops will maintain safety despite acknowledging multiple system vulnerabilities.

- Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy: Relevance Fallacy — Finding patterns in emergent dynamics after observing outcomes rather than predicting them beforehand.

- Nirvana Fallacy: Informal Fallacy — Dismissing concerns about imperfect safety mechanisms while acknowledging they have real serious limits.

- Incomplete Comparison: False Attribution — Comparing AI transparency favorably to Cold War secrecy without addressing effectiveness differences.

- Kettle Logic: Informal Fallacy — Simultaneously arguing risks are acute and accelerating while system resilience is holding up well.

- Appeal to Emotion: Red Herring — Using dramatic language about machines walking through us to support technical arguments.

- Fallacy of Composition: Correlative-based Fallacy — Assuming individual lab transparency creates industry-wide safety without demonstrating collective sufficiency.

- Argument from Silence: Relevance Fallacy — Suggesting Chinese labs lack safety infrastructure because they don't publish research publicly.
