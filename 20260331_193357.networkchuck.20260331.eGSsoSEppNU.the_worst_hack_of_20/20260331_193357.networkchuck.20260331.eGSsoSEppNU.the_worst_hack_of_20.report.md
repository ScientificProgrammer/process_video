## Executive Summary

# Critical NPM Supply Chain Attack: Axios Hijacked

## Overview

Axios, the most popular HTTP library in the JavaScript ecosystem with over 100 million weekly downloads, suffered a sophisticated supply chain attack. Attackers hijacked a maintainer's account to inject a Remote Access Trojan (RAT) that deploys in 1.1 seconds and erases all traces of itself. This may be one of the most dangerous supply chain attacks in history, potentially affecting anyone who ran `npm install` during the compromise window.

---

## Understanding the Attack Vector

### What is Axios and Why It Matters

- **Axios** is an HTTP library enabling code to communicate with the internet
- Most developers have Axios installed as a **dependency** without directly installing it
- The average NPM project trusts **200 to 2,100 strangers** with code execution
- **174,000 projects** depend on Axios, making it a high-value target
- Axios is maintained by unpaid open-source contributors

### The Trust Chain Problem

- Installing a package means trusting **all dependencies** that package relies on
- This creates a chain of trust extending to hundreds of unknown developers
- Supply chain attacks exploit this trust by compromising upstream dependencies

---

## The Breach: How It Happened

### Initial Compromise

- **Jason Semen**, a lead Axios maintainer, had his account compromised
- The attacker obtained his **long-lived NPM classic access token**
- The method of initial compromise remains unknown
- Attacker changed the account email to: `ifstoppro.mme`

### The Clever Injection Method

- The attacker **never directly added malicious code** to Axios source files
- Instead, they added **one line** to the `package.json` file: a dependency called `plain-crypto.js`
- This dependency was never imported into any of Axios's 86 source files
- It existed solely to run its **postinstall script** automatically

### Bypassing Security Measures

- The attacker staged a **clean version** of the file 18 hours before the malicious one
- They used **npm CLI directly** to bypass CI/CD pipelines and guardrails
- **Socket.dev** was the first to discover the attack
- Two release branches were compromised: **1.14.1** and **0.30.4**
- Both versions were poisoned within **39 minutes** of each other

---

## How the Malware Works

### The Attack Sequence

**Step 1: Automatic Execution**
- When `npm install` runs, the postinstall script executes automatically
- No user action required beyond the install command

**Step 2: The Dropper**
- The script triggers a **dropper** that deploys `setup.js`
- Appears harmless (looks like math operations)
- Contains **two layers of obfuscation** using XOR and Base64 encoding
- Uses a mysterious "order 7077" phrase for additional obfuscation

**Step 3: Deployment (1.1 seconds)**
- `setup.js` deobfuscates the malicious payload
- Detects the victim's **operating system** (Mac, Windows, or Linux)
- Contacts the **C2 (Command and Control) server**
- Downloads the appropriate **RAT (Remote Access Trojan)** for the detected OS

**Step 4: Cleanup and Stealth**
- Deletes `setup.js` to remove evidence
- Deletes the malicious `package.json`
- Renames the pre-staged clean `package.md` to `package.json`
- Leaves **no visible trace** of the infection

### The Supply Chain Analogy

The video uses a coffee poisoning analogy to explain supply chain attacks:
- **Direct attack**: Poisoning your coffee cup (hardest, most visible)
- **Roaster level**: Poisoning beans at the roaster (affects all customers)
- **Supplier level**: Poisoning bags used by all roasters (wider reach)
- **Water supply**: Poisoning the water source (affects everyone, not just coffee drinkers)

The Axios attack represents the "water supply" level of compromise in the JavaScript ecosystem.

**CLAIM REFUTATION EVIDENCE:**

**LOGICAL FALLACIES:**
