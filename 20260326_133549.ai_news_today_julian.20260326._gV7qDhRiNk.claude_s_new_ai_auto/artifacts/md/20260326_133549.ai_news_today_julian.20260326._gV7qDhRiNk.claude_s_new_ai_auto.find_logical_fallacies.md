# FALLACIES

- Appeal to Popularity: Relevance Fallacy — Using 2.4 million views and social media engagement as evidence of validity or importance.

- Bandwagon Argument: Relevance Fallacy — Claiming "almost everyone was using" dangerously skip permissions implies correctness through widespread adoption.

- Hasty Generalization: Faulty Generalization — Extrapolating from one developer's X post with 2,000 likes to claim universal behavior patterns.

- Argument from Anecdote: Faulty Generalization — Using individual social media replies as evidence for broad claims about user behavior.

- False Dilemma: Informal Fallacy — Presenting only two options: approve every action or turn off safety completely, ignoring alternatives.

- Appeal to Fear: Appeal to Emotion — Describing catastrophic scenarios like home directory deletion to manipulate emotional response toward solution.

- Appeal to Authority: Relevance Fallacy — Citing unnamed "security researchers," "one analysis," and unverified surveys without credible sources.

- Cherry Picking: Faulty Generalization — Selectively presenting positive examples while downplaying security vulnerabilities until later in presentation.

- Misleading Vividness: Faulty Generalization — Using vivid description of 45-minute agency work to suggest exceptional cases are typical.

- Slippery Slope: Informal Fallacy — Suggesting people who don't adopt now will be left behind in 12 months inevitably.

- Appeal to Novelty: Relevance Fallacy — Implying auto mode is superior simply because it's new and recently released technology.

- Argument from Ignorance: Relevance Fallacy — Claiming non-technical users "have no way to know" command safety, ignoring learning resources.

- False Equivalence: Informal Fallacy — Equating code generation capability with complete professional web development service delivery and value.

- Inflation of Conflict: Informal Fallacy — Acknowledging security vulnerabilities but minimizing their significance to maintain positive narrative about product.

- Appeal to Consequences: Relevance Fallacy — Arguing auto mode is good because it removes friction, regardless of security implications.

- Proof by Assertion: Informal Fallacy — Repeatedly stating "this is real" and "this is happening now" without independent verification.

- Ipse Dixit: Relevance Fallacy — Making claims about revenue figures and survey results without supporting documentation or sources.

- Moving the Goalposts: Informal Fallacy — First claiming auto mode solves safety, then admitting vulnerabilities exist but arguing it's still better.

- Appeal to Wealth: Relevance Fallacy — Using Anthropic's $60 billion valuation and revenue figures to imply product quality or trustworthiness.

- Misleading Vividness: Faulty Generalization — Using specific incident of deleted home directory as emotional anchor while claiming system improvements.

- Argument from Repetition: Relevance Fallacy — Repeatedly promoting AI Profit Boardroom with same membership numbers to normalize the sales pitch.

- Conflict of Interest (Undisclosed): Relevance Fallacy — Promoting own paid service while presenting as objective analysis of technology without disclosure.

- False Cause: Questionable Cause — Implying early adoption causes success rather than other factors like skill or resources.

- Appeal to FOMO: Appeal to Emotion — Creating urgency by suggesting people will "look very different" if they don't join now.

- Thought-Terminating Cliche: Informal Fallacy — "That's the whole game right now" ends critical thinking about whether claims are accurate.

- Package Deal: Informal Fallacy — Bundling legitimate technical features with unverified business claims as if equally credible.

- Texas Sharpshooter Fallacy: Relevance Fallacy — Selecting specific positive examples while ignoring broader data about actual adoption and success rates.

- Nirvana Fallacy: Informal Fallacy — Dismissing old permission system as complete failure because it wasn't perfect solution.

- Appeal to Novelty: Relevance Fallacy — Suggesting new service category of "Claude Code experts" is inherently valuable because it's new.

- Incomplete Comparison: Informal Fallacy — Comparing Claude Code quality to competitors without specifying which competitors or methodology used.
