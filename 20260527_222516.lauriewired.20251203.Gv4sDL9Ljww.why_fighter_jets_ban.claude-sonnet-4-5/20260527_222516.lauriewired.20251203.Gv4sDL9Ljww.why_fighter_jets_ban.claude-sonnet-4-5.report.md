## Core Analysis

### Claims Analysis

**ARGUMENT SUMMARY:**

Safety-critical aerospace software like F-35 uses restricted C++ (JSF standard) that removes dangerous features like exceptions, dynamic memory allocation, and recursion to ensure reliability.

**TRUTH CLAIMS:**

**CLAIM 1:** Ariane 5 rocket crashed on June 4, 1996 due to unhandled software exception.

**SUPPORT:** The European Space Agency's official inquiry confirms the Ariane 5 Flight 501 failure on June 4, 1996, was caused by an overflow error when converting a 64-bit floating point number to a 16-bit signed integer in the Inertial Reference System software. The failure occurred 36.7 seconds after launch, destroying the rocket and its payload valued at approximately $500 million. IEEE Spectrum and other technical publications documented this as one of the most expensive software bugs in history.

**REFUTATION:** The specific timing of "36 seconds" is slightly imprecise; official reports state 36.7 seconds. Cost figures vary depending on whether development costs are included, though the order of magnitude is generally accepted.

**RATING:** A (Definitely True)

**LABELS:** Factual, technical, historical, well-documented

---

**CLAIM 2:** F-35 JSF C++ standard prohibits use of exceptions (AV rule 208).

**SUPPORT:** The publicly available "Joint Strike Fighter Air Vehicle C++ Coding Standards" document (Lockheed Martin Corporation, December 2005) explicitly states in AV Rule 208: "C++ exceptions shall not be used." This document is widely cited in aerospace software engineering literature and is publicly accessible online. Multiple aerospace software engineering textbooks reference this specific prohibition.

**RATING:** A (Definitely True)

**LABELS:** Factual, technical, verifiable, documented

---

**CLAIM 3:** The first microprocessor was created by Garrett AiResearch for F-14, not Intel.

**SUPPORT:** The Garrett AiResearch CADC (Central Air Data Computer) used in the F-14 was developed in the late 1960s and incorporated microprocessor technology. Ray Holt documented the MP944 chip set was operational before Intel's 4004. The technology was classified until 1998.

**REFUTATION:** The definition of "microprocessor" is contested. Intel's 4004 (1971) is widely recognized as the first single-chip microprocessor, while the Garrett system used a multi-chip architecture. The Computer History Museum and most authoritative computing histories credit Intel with the first commercial microprocessor. The claim depends on how "microprocessor" is defined—single-chip versus multi-chip implementation, commercial versus military application.

**LOGICAL FALLACIES:** Equivocation—using different definitions of "microprocessor" to make the claim.

**RATING:** C (Medium)

**LABELS:** Contested, definitional, technical, requires-context

---

**CLAIM 4:** F-4 Phantom had essentially no software, using mechanical bombing computers instead.

**SUPPORT:** Historical documentation confirms early F-4 variants used analog mechanical computing systems for weapons delivery. The AN/ASQ-91 weapons release computer system used in early F-4s was largely mechanical with cams and gears. DoD historical documents describe early 1960s-1970s fighter aircraft as having minimal digital computing capability.

**REFUTATION:** Later F-4 variants (particularly F-4E and beyond) incorporated digital systems and avionics computers. The claim oversimplifies by not distinguishing between early and late F-4 variants produced over a 20+ year period.

**LOGICAL FALLACIES:** Overgeneralization—treating all F-4 variants as identical when discussing software content.

**RATING:** B (High)

**LABELS:** Generally-accurate, requires-nuance, historical, technical

---

**CLAIM 5:** F-35 contains 9 million lines of onboard code.

**SUPPORT:** Multiple defense industry sources and Lockheed Martin documentation cite figures of 8-9 million lines of code for the F-35's onboard systems. A 2009 IEEE Spectrum article cited approximately 5.7 million lines for the aircraft and 3.5 million for ground systems, totaling over 9 million. Various defense publications and congressional testimonies reference similar figures.

**REFUTATION:** The exact number varies by source and may depend on what software is counted (onboard only vs. total system including ground support). Some sources cite different figures ranging from 8-24 million lines when including all related systems. Software lines of code continue to grow with updates, making any specific number time-dependent.

**RATING:** B (High)

**LABELS:** Generally-accurate, approximate, technical, contextual

---

**CLAIM 6:** Department of Defense mandated Ada usage for approximately 15 years starting in 1980s.

**SUPPORT:** DoD Directive 3405.1 (1987) and DoD-STD-2167 required Ada for mission-critical systems unless a waiver was granted. The Ada mandate was officially in place from the mid-1980s until it was lifted in 1997 with the revision to MIL-STD-498. Numerous government and academic sources document this approximately 12-15 year period of Ada mandate.

**REFUTATION:** The mandate was lifted in 1997, making the duration closer to 10-12 years depending on when enforcement truly began, though "approximately 15 years" is reasonable. The mandate had numerous waivers and exceptions even during its enforcement period.

**RATING:** A (Definitely True)

**LABELS:** Historical, factual, documented, government-policy

---

**CLAIM 7:** Bjarne Stroustrup contributed to formulation of JSF C++ rules.

**SUPPORT:** Stroustrup has publicly acknowledged involvement in discussions about the JSF C++ coding standards in interviews and writings. The JSF coding standards document acknowledgments and various aerospace software engineering publications reference consultation with C++ language experts including Stroustrup.

**REFUTATION:** The exact extent of Stroustrup's involvement is not fully documented in publicly available sources.

**RATING:** B (High)

**LABELS:** Likely-accurate, expert-involvement, qualified-claim

---

**CLAIM 8:** Ada compilers cost thousands of dollars in the 1990s.

**SUPPORT:** Historical documentation shows commercial Ada compilers in the 1980s-1990s were expensive, often costing several thousand dollars per license. This was common for specialized language compilers before the open-source movement gained momentum.

**REFUTATION:** GNAT (GNU Ada Translator) became available as a free Ada compiler in the mid-1990s, though commercial versions still cost money. The timeframe when "all" Ada compilers were expensive was relatively brief before free alternatives emerged.

**LOGICAL FALLACIES:** Hasty generalization—suggesting all Ada compilers were expensive without noting free alternatives that emerged.

**RATING:** B (High)

**LABELS:** Generally-accurate, historical, economic, contextual
